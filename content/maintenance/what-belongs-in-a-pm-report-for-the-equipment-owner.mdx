An equipment owner does not need a blank PM checklist after a service visit. They need a clear record of the work that happened to their asset. The best report is short enough to use and specific enough to answer four questions.

## 1. What was checked?

Start with the equipment identity and the context for the visit: the asset or unit ID, date, technician, and relevant meter or odometer reading. Then group the checked systems in a way that lets an owner understand the scope without asking the technician to reconstruct it later.

This is not a claim that every system passed a universal standard. It is a record of the work that was actually performed on this equipment during this visit.

## 2. What was found?

Findings should be legible to a non-technician without erasing the technician's detail. A useful report separates a normal observation from an item needing attention, and gives enough context for the owner to decide whether follow-up needs scheduling.

Where a measurement, photograph, or note matters, preserve it with the finding. The goal is not more fields; it is an account that still makes sense after the equipment has left the shop.

## 3. What was done?

Record completed work plainly: service performed, parts or materials used when relevant, adjustments made, and work intentionally deferred. This is the section that turns an inspection-shaped form into a service record an owner can use in conversations with their team and future providers.

## 4. What comes next?

Finish with the follow-up context: work recommended, an item to monitor, and the time, meter, or operating information that helps plan the next visit. The equipment manufacturer’s service documentation remains the source for the actual interval and procedure.

## Why this matters to the technician

The record is part of the service. A clear owner copy shows that the technician documented the visit, communicated its outcome, and made the next step understandable. It does not promise a business outcome, and it does not make a compliance claim. It simply gives the owner a usable account of the work.

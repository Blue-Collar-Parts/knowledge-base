**Regeneration burns soot out of a diesel particulate filter while it stays on the engine. Cleaning removes ash, which regeneration can't touch, and it requires taking the filter off. A dashboard regen prompt and a filter that needs cleaning are different problems, and mixing them up is a common point of confusion.** See [DPF cleaning and regeneration](/kb/dpf-cleaning-and-regeneration) for how the two fit into the aftertreatment system as a whole.

## Passive regeneration

Passive regeneration happens on its own, without any driver or operator action, when exhaust temperature is naturally high enough — sustained highway speed and load, for example — to oxidize the soot the filter has collected. The operator typically never knows it happened. Equipment that runs mostly at low load or idle gets less passive regeneration, which is why that duty cycle tends to need active or parked regeneration more often.

## Active regeneration

When exhaust temperature isn't naturally high enough and soot load reaches a threshold the engine control system is watching for, the engine itself raises exhaust temperature — through strategies such as extra fuel injection or intake throttling, managed by the engine control module — to trigger regeneration while the vehicle keeps working. This also usually happens without the operator doing anything, though some equipment shows a dash indication that it's underway.

## Parked (stationary) regeneration

If soot load keeps climbing because active regeneration hasn't been able to keep up — short-cycle driving, extended idle, or a derate close behind — the system calls for a parked regeneration: the vehicle or equipment is stopped, typically in park or neutral with the brake set, and regeneration is commanded and runs to completion before work resumes. The switch, the dash messages and the procedure differ by engine and by on-highway or off-highway application, so follow the manufacturer's documentation for the specific engine, serial number and build. Exhaust components stay hot after a regeneration ends, so keep the area clear as that documentation directs.

## Signs the filter needs to come off, not just regenerate

A filter that keeps calling for parked regenerations more often than it used to, that regenerates but still shows reduced power or a soot-load warning soon after, or that triggers a service lamp indicating the filter is at or near its ash limit, is a candidate for removal and ash cleaning rather than another regeneration cycle. The manufacturer's documentation for the specific engine and filter sets the ash-load threshold and the interval at which cleaning is expected; a service provider doesn't estimate it from symptoms alone. See [recording a DPF service](/kb/recording-a-dpf-service) for what to capture when that decision is made.

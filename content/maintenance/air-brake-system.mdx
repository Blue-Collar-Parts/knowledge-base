Air brakes stop a semi truck and trailer with compressed air instead of hydraulic fluid. An engine-driven compressor fills storage tanks. When the driver presses the brake pedal, a valve sends air from those tanks to a brake chamber at each wheel. The chamber pushes a rod that applies the brake. On a drum brake, the rod turns a slack adjuster and the shoes press against the drum. On a disc brake, the pads clamp the rotor. When the driver lets go of the pedal, the air is let out and the brakes release.

## How do air brakes work?

Air brakes work by storing energy as compressed air and releasing it into brake chambers on command. The system has three stages.

1. **Supply.** The compressor builds air pressure. The governor tells it when to build and when to stop. The air dryer takes moisture out, and the reservoirs (air tanks) store the air until it is needed.
2. **Control.** The driver's foot valve, called the treadle valve, meters air toward the brakes in proportion to how hard the pedal is pressed. Relay valves near the axles send air from a nearby tank straight to the chambers, so the brakes at the back of a long combination do not lag behind the ones up front. Quick-release valves let that air out close to the chambers when the pedal comes up.
3. **Foundation brakes.** At each wheel end, a brake chamber turns air pressure into a push on a rod. On a drum brake, the slack adjuster turns that push into rotation of the brake camshaft and the shoes press on the drum. On an air disc brake, the chamber works the caliper and the pads clamp the rotor.

Two more systems sit on top of those stages. **Spring brakes** give the truck its parking and emergency brakes, and **antilock brakes (ABS)** keep a wheel from locking under hard braking. Both are covered below.

## How are air brakes different from hydraulic brakes?

Hydraulic brakes, like those on a car or a light truck, push a sealed column of fluid from a master cylinder to each wheel, and the driver's foot, usually helped by a booster, supplies the force. Air brakes use stored air, so the force comes from the tanks, not the driver's leg. That is what lets one pedal stop a fully loaded combination.

The two systems also fail differently. In an air system, the compressor makes up for a small leak until the leak outruns it. And the system is designed so that losing air applies the spring brakes rather than leaving the truck with none. Air lines are also easy to connect and disconnect between a tractor and a trailer, which a sealed fluid system is not.

## What are the parts of an air brake system?

An air brake system has a supply side that makes and stores air, valves that control where it goes, and foundation brakes at each wheel that turn it into stopping force.

### Air compressor and governor

The compressor is driven by the engine, by gear or belt, and turns whenever the engine runs. The governor watches tank pressure. When pressure reaches the top of its range, the governor unloads the compressor so it stops pumping air into the system. When pressure falls to the bottom of its range, the governor loads it again. The cut-in and cut-out pressures are set by the governor's manufacturer; check its documentation for the unit in front of you.

### Air dryer

Compressed air carries water and some compressor oil. The air dryer sits between the compressor and the tanks and passes the air through a desiccant cartridge that holds the moisture, purging what it collected each time the compressor unloads. Water that gets past a failing dryer collects in the tanks and lines, where it corrodes parts and can freeze.

### Reservoirs and drain valves

The reservoirs store the air. A tractor normally has a supply tank that takes air first, from the dryer, and a separate tank for each brake circuit, with one-way check valves between them so a leak in one does not drain the others. Each tank has a drain valve for releasing collected water and oil. Some are drained by hand and some by an automatic drain valve.

### Treadle valve, relay valves and quick-release valves

The treadle valve under the brake pedal is the driver's control. On most trucks it is a dual valve, with one section for each brake circuit. Relay valves sit near the axles they serve. A small signal from the treadle valve opens the relay valve, which feeds air from a nearby tank to the chambers. Quick-release valves let that air out near the chambers, so the brakes release quickly instead of waiting for the air to travel back to the cab.

### Brake chambers

A brake chamber is a round metal housing with a rubber diaphragm inside. Air enters behind the diaphragm and pushes it, and the diaphragm pushes a rod out of the chamber. How far the rod travels is called pushrod stroke. A worn lining, or a slack adjuster that is not keeping up, shows up as longer stroke.

### Slack adjusters

The slack adjuster connects the pushrod to the brake camshaft. It is a lever: the pushrod moves its arm, and the arm rotates the camshaft. As linings wear, the brake needs more stroke to apply, so the slack adjuster takes up that slack. Modern trucks use automatic slack adjusters, which do this on their own. A brake that keeps going out of adjustment is a symptom, usually of a worn foundation brake part or a failing adjuster, and adjusting it by hand does not fix the cause.

### Drum brakes and air disc brakes

Most heavy trucks and trailers use S-cam drum brakes. The S-shaped cam at the end of the camshaft spreads the brake shoes against the inside of the drum. Air disc brakes are increasingly common. They clamp pads against a rotor and adjust themselves inside the caliper, with no external slack adjuster.

### Spring brakes and the parking brake

The parking and emergency brakes use a spring brake chamber, a second chamber mounted behind the service chamber. Inside is a powerful spring. Air pressure holds the spring compressed, and the brake stays released. When the driver sets the parking brake, or when the air pressure drops far enough, the air is let out and the spring applies the brake. That is the design intent: a truck that loses its air is held by its spring brakes rather than left free to roll. A spring brake chamber stores a great deal of energy, and it must never be opened or worked on without the manufacturer's procedure.

### Antilock brakes (ABS)

ABS uses a wheel speed sensor at each monitored wheel and an electronic controller. When a wheel starts to lock, the controller has a modulator valve release and reapply air to that brake, many times a second, so the wheel keeps turning and the driver can still steer. A warning lamp tells the driver when the system finds a fault. The tractor and the trailer each have their own ABS and their own lamp.

## What is a dual air brake system?

A dual air brake system splits the brakes into two independent circuits, each with its own tank and its own section of the treadle valve. On most tractors one circuit, called primary, serves the rear axles, and the other, called secondary, serves the steer axle. If one circuit loses air, the other is designed to keep braking the truck, so a single failed hose should not take out every brake.

## What does the low air warning do?

The low air warning is a light, and usually a buzzer, in the cab. It comes on when the pressure in either brake circuit falls below a set level. It tells the driver to stop safely before the pressure falls far enough for the spring brakes to apply on their own. The vehicle manufacturer sets the warning point, and the air gauges in the cab show the pressure in each circuit.

## How do the trailer's air brakes connect to the tractor?

Two air lines run from the back of the cab to the front of the trailer, and they couple at gladhands. The **supply line**, also called the emergency line and usually red, fills the trailer's tanks and holds off the trailer's spring brakes. The **service line**, usually blue, carries the brake signal: when the driver brakes, air pressure in this line tells the trailer's relay valve to apply the trailer's brakes from the trailer's own tanks.

On a tractor's dash, a yellow control sets the parking brakes, and a red control charges or cuts off the trailer's air supply. Cutting off the supply applies the trailer's brakes. If the trailer breaks away, or the supply line is torn, the trailer's brakes apply on their own, from the trailer's own tank or its spring brakes. As supply pressure falls, the tractor-protection valve closes off both lines, so the tractor keeps its own air.

## Which air brake parts do roadside inspectors cite most?

**Among units cited for a brake violation at roadside, 29.7% were cited for ABS, 24.4% for air hoses and tubing, 24.3% for air leaks and 18.5% for a brake out of adjustment (n = 522,922 cited units).** Hoses or leaks, or both, appeared on 45.5% of them. These are counts of what inspectors recorded, computed by Blue Collar Parts from FMCSA's public data.

| Parking brake | 9,549 | 1.8% |
| Low air warning device | 6,369 | 1.2% |
| Reservoirs, compressor, check and drain valves | 5,581 | 1.1% |

How these numbers were made:

- **Dataset:** FMCSA vehicle inspection data, public roadside inspections, one current version per inspection.
- **Window:** inspections from 2025-10-19 to 2026-10-18.
- **Unit:** one inspected power unit or trailer in one inspection. A unit inspected twice counts twice.
- **Population:** units with at least one violation in FMCSA's two brake categories, "Brakes, Out of Adjustment" and "Brakes, All Other Violations". Units inspected with no brake violation are not in this data.
- **What the number is:** a mix among units cited for brakes. It is not a rate and not a likelihood. It says what those units were cited for, not how likely any truck is to be cited.
- **Groups:** each row is a family of FMCSA violation codes grouped by Blue Collar Parts. A unit can be in several rows, and the two chamber-leak codes count as both a leak and a chamber defect, so the shares add to more than 100%. No row covers the governor or the air dryer, because the brake categories have no separate code for them.

These numbers describe what inspectors recorded. They are not a safety rating and not a finding about any carrier or truck, and inspectors choose which units to stop, so these units are not a sample of all trucks on the road.

## Before you work on air brakes

Compressed air and spring brakes both store energy. A brake that looks released can still hold a loaded spring, and a line that looks empty can still hold pressure. Follow the manufacturer's service documentation for the specific component, and use the safe-work steps it calls for before loosening anything.

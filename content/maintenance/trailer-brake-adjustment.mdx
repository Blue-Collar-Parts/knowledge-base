Trailer brake adjustment is checked the same way as any air brake, but the trailer side of the system has its own plumbing and its own failure points that the tractor doesn't have. This page covers the trailer's part of the [air brake system](/kb/air-brake-system): the lines that feed it, the valve that controls it, and why its brakes drift out of adjustment.

## The trailer's air supply

A trailer gets its air through two lines from the tractor, connected at the gladhand couplings: the **supply (emergency) line**, which keeps the trailer's reservoirs charged and the spring brakes released, and the **service line**, which carries the signal to apply the brakes. A damaged gladhand seal, a kinked or chafed line, or a coupling that isn't fully seated shows up as an air leak, a slow brake response, or — if the supply line loses pressure — the trailer's spring brakes applying on their own.

## The relay-emergency valve

The **relay-emergency valve** sits at the front of the trailer and does two jobs: it relays the service-line signal to the trailer's own reservoir air so each brake application doesn't have to travel the full length of the trailer, and it applies the trailer's spring brakes automatically if the supply line loses pressure, whether from a parted line or an intentional tractor protection valve action. A relay-emergency valve that's slow to respond, that leaks, or that doesn't release fully affects every brake behind it, not just one wheel end.

## Spring brakes on a trailer

Like the tractor, a trailer's spring brake chambers hold the parking and emergency brake applied by spring force and released by air pressure. See [brake chamber air leaks and replacement](/kb/brake-chamber-air-leaks) for what to check at the chamber itself. A spring brake that doesn't release fully, or that releases unevenly axle to axle, will show up as a brake that's hard to adjust correctly even when the adjuster and foundation brake are sound.

## Checking adjustment on a trailer

Checking a trailer brake's adjustment is the same measurement as on a tractor — stroke at the brake chamber, checked against the limit for that chamber type — but a trailer usually has more axles and more wheel ends to check in one pass, and they don't all see the same wear. The measurement itself, and the limits that decide pass or fail at a federal annual inspection, are on [Appendix A inspection standards](/kb/appendix-a-inspection-standards). This page doesn't restate those limits.

## Why a trailer brake goes out of adjustment

An automatic slack adjuster is built to take up wear on its own, so a trailer brake that's drifted out of adjustment is usually a symptom of something else: a seized or binding adjuster, corrosion or water intrusion at a wheel end that spends more time sitting between loads than a tractor does, a worn camshaft bushing, or a foundation brake component that's worn or damaged. [Slack adjuster adjustment](/kb/slack-adjuster-adjustment) covers how automatic and manual adjusters work and why to find the cause rather than just readjusting. This page stays on what's specific to the trailer: the supply side, the relay-emergency valve, and the spring brakes that make a trailer's air brake system different from a single-unit truck's.

## What should the service record show?

A record of trailer brake work should show:

- which axle and wheel end each finding belongs to;
- what was checked: supply and service lines, gladhands, the relay-emergency valve, spring brakes, adjusters;
- the brake adjustment found at each wheel end, as measured, and what was done about it;
- the cause found when a brake was out of adjustment, not only the readjustment;
- parts replaced, with part numbers.

A record kept by the trailer's own unit number or VIN stays with the trailer, whichever tractor pulled it.

A marine diesel engine maintenance checklist organizes a preventive maintenance (PM) visit by system, so the technician works through the engine and its supporting equipment in a consistent order and the record reads the same way every time. The specific items, procedures, and service intervals come from the engine and gear manufacturer's documentation, not from a generic checklist.

This page describes what a marine diesel PM covers at the category level. It is a map of the visit, not a replacement for the service manual.

## What a marine diesel PM covers

Marine service puts an engine through long idle periods, humid air, and water that can be salty. A PM checklist for a marine diesel groups the visit into seven systems.

1. **Engine oil and filter.** Oil and its filter are the engine's first line of defense, and marine duty cycles can be hard on both. Recording their condition gives the next technician a baseline to compare against.
2. **Fuel system, primary filter and water separator.** Clean fuel matters on a boat, where water and condensation can end up in the tank and the primary filter. This category covers the fuel path from the tank through the first stage of filtration.
3. **Raw water and coolant system.** Many marine engines are cooled by water drawn from outside the hull, backed by a coolant loop inside the engine. Both circuits have to keep moving and keep their protection in place, so the PM looks at each one.
4. **Gearbox oil and transmission.** The marine gear carries the engine's output to the propeller. Its fluid and seals deserve their own line in the record, separate from the engine.
5. **Exhaust system and elbow.** Where hot exhaust meets cooling water, metal wears and corrodes. This category covers the exhaust path and the cooling lines connected to it.
6. **Belts and hoses.** Belts drive pumps and accessories, and hoses carry coolant, raw water, and intake air. They age quietly, so a scheduled look is how wear gets caught before it becomes a failure.
7. **Corrosion and anodes.** Sacrificial anodes are designed to wear away so other metal does not. Checking them, along with the general effects of a marine environment, rounds out the visit.

Each category is a heading in the technician's record. What gets checked under each one, how often, and what counts as acceptable all depend on the specific engine and gear, so follow the manufacturer's documentation for those details.

## What your customer should receive

A completed PM is only useful if the customer can see it. A good record shows what was checked, what was found, what work was done, and what to plan for next, organized by the same systems the technician worked through.

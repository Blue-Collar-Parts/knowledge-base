**An EGR (exhaust gas recirculation) cooler lowers the temperature of exhaust gas before it's routed back into the engine's intake, which reduces combustion temperature and the NOx the engine produces in the first place.** It sits upstream of the DPF and SCR, and when it leaks or clogs, the effects can look like they belong somewhere else in the engine. See [DPF cleaning and regeneration](/kb/dpf-cleaning-and-regeneration) for where EGR fits into the full aftertreatment system.

## What the cooler does

EGR routes a portion of exhaust gas back into the intake air stream instead of out the tailpipe. Because that recirculated gas is very hot, it's passed through a cooler — typically a coolant-to-exhaust-gas heat exchanger — before it reaches the intake. The cooler has to handle sustained high temperatures and a corrosive gas stream on one side and engine coolant on the other, which is why it's a wear item rather than something expected to last the life of the engine.

## General signs of a failing EGR cooler

These are concepts to watch for, not a diagnostic procedure — confirm any of them against the manufacturer's service documentation for the specific engine:

- **Unexplained coolant loss.** A cracked or leaking cooler lets coolant into the exhaust or intake side, so the loss doesn't show up as an external puddle the way a hose leak would.
- **White exhaust smoke.** Coolant entering the combustion chamber or exhaust stream through a failed cooler can produce white smoke, particularly on startup or under load.
- **Overheating.** Coolant loss, or coolant mixing with combustion gases, can push engine temperature up even when other cooling system components check out fine.
- **Contaminated intake air.** Exhaust soot and condensation getting past a failing cooler can foul the intake tract and, over time, sensors and components downstream of it.

Any of these can also point to other causes — a head gasket, a radiator, a different cooling system component — so EGR cooler failure is a hypothesis to confirm, not a conclusion to jump to from one symptom alone.

## How to tell an EGR cooler apart from a similar-looking failure

Two checks separate a failing EGR cooler from the other things that cause the same symptoms. A cooling-system pressure test holds the system at pressure to find an external or internal leak; if pressure drops with no external leak visible, combustion gas or exhaust is likely entering the coolant somewhere, which an EGR cooler, a head gasket, or a cracked cylinder head can all cause. A combustion leak test on the coolant (sometimes called a block test) checks specifically for combustion gases in the coolant, which narrows the leak to the combustion side rather than a plain coolant leak. Deposits in the intake can add a clue, but not a verdict: soot and oil residue can come from the EGR system, a turbocharger seal, crankcase ventilation or more than one of these at once, so confirm with the manufacturer's diagnostic procedure before tearing into any of them.

## Why replacement is a documented repair, not routine maintenance

An EGR cooler replacement is diagnosed and performed following the manufacturer's procedure for that specific engine, which covers coolant system service, torque specifications, and any related components replaced at the same time, such as gaskets or the EGR valve. Follow that documentation rather than a general description; this page doesn't reproduce procedure steps.

## What to record

- **Complaint, cause and correction** for the repair — see [complaint, cause, correction](/kb/complaint-cause-correction) for the structure.
- **What pointed to the EGR cooler**: the coolant loss, smoke, overheating, or intake contamination observed, and any pressure test or diagnostic reading that confirmed it.
- **The part replaced** and any related components serviced at the same time.
- **Verification**: how the repair was confirmed — a pressure test with no leak found, coolant level holding over a test run, smoke no longer present.

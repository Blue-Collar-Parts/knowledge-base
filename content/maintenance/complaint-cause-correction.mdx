**Complaint, cause, correction** (the "3 C's") is how technicians write up a repair or service job. Written well, the three lines tell the customer the whole job in the order it happened.

- **Complaint:** what was reported.
- **Cause:** what the technician found.
- **Correction:** the repair actually performed, and how it was confirmed to work.

## What does a complaint, cause, correction template look like?

Three labeled lines, one per C:

> **Complaint:** (what was reported, and under what conditions)
>
> **Cause:** (what you found, and the test or check that showed it)
>
> **Correction:** (the repair or service performed, the parts used, and how you verified it)

The sections below say what goes in each line.

## Complaint: what was reported

Write the problem the way it was brought to you, with the conditions that go with it. Who reported it, when it happens, and under what load, speed, temperature, or operating condition.

- Keep the customer's or operator's description, even if you would phrase it differently.
- Add the conditions you were told about. "Loses power climbing grades when loaded" points somewhere different than "loses power."
- For scheduled work with no reported problem, the complaint line states the request, such as the scheduled preventive maintenance service.

## Cause: what you found

The cause is the diagnosis, not a restatement of the complaint. It answers *why* the complaint happened.

- Name the test or check that led you there, and the result. A reading, an observation, or a photo belongs here when it settled the diagnosis.
- Separate the root cause from the symptom. A blown fuse can be the symptom of a chafed wire.
- If you could not reproduce the complaint, say so and say what you checked. "Could not duplicate" with nothing after it is not a cause.

## Correction: what you did

Record the work performed, plainly.

- The repair or service performed and the parts or materials used.
- **How you verified the repair.** A road test, a test run under load, a re-check of the reading that pointed to the cause. Verification is what turns "replaced the part" into "fixed the problem."
- Anything found but not fixed, and why: declined by the customer, parts on order, or outside the scope of the visit. Deferred work that is written down is work the customer can plan for.

## An example write-up

*Illustrative only, not a real job, and not a procedure for any make or model.*

> **Complaint:** Operator reports the generator shuts down after about 20 minutes under load.
>
> **Cause:** Found coolant level low. Pressure-tested the cooling system and found a leak at the lower radiator hose clamp. The engine shut down on high coolant temperature.
>
> **Correction:** Replaced the lower hose clamp and refilled coolant per the manufacturer's service documentation. Pressure-tested the system again with no leak found. Ran the unit under load for 45 minutes with no shutdown and normal operating temperature.

Each line does one job. Someone who was not there can tell what happened, why, and how it was confirmed.

## How do you write the correction line?

Say what you did, what you used, and how you know it worked, in that order. A correction line that stops at "replaced part" or "repaired" leaves the customer to guess whether the problem is gone.

A pattern that fits most jobs:

> **Correction:** (Replaced, repaired or adjusted) (what), using (parts or materials). Verified by (the test, run or re-check), with (the result).

The lines below show that pattern on common jobs. *They are illustrative wording, not repair procedures. Torque values, refrigerant type and charge, calibration steps and service intervals come from the manufacturer's service documentation for the unit.*

### Cylinder head and head gasket

> **Correction:** Removed the cylinder head and replaced the head gasket. Had the head checked for flatness and cracks before refitting it. Fitted new head bolts where the manufacturer requires them, and torqued them to the manufacturer's specification and sequence. Refilled and bled the cooling system. Verified by a cooling system pressure test with no leak, then ran the engine to operating temperature with no coolant loss and no combustion gas in the coolant.

### A/C evaporator clean and recharge

> **Correction:** Cleaned the evaporator core. Recovered the refrigerant, evacuated the system and confirmed it held vacuum, then recharged with the refrigerant type and amount on the unit's charge label. Verified by checking vent outlet temperature and system pressures against the manufacturer's range at the specified test conditions.

### Actuator replacement

> **Correction:** Replaced the turbocharger actuator (part number recorded on the work order). Ran the actuator calibration with the diagnostic software where the manufacturer's procedure calls for it. Cleared the fault codes. Verified by a loaded road test under the conditions in the complaint, with no active or pending codes afterward and boost readings in the live data within the manufacturer's expected range.

### Full PM service

> **Complaint:** Scheduled preventive maintenance service.
>
> **Correction:** Completed the scheduled PM on the customer's PM checklist (attached). Changed the engine oil, oil filters and fuel filters; lubricated the chassis; checked brakes, tires, lights and fluid levels and recorded the readings on the checklist. Found the left front brake lining close to the replacement point; quoted it, and the customer deferred it to the next visit.

On a PM, the correction line points to the checklist for the readings and names anything found but not fixed.

## How do you show the fix was verified?

Re-check under the conditions the complaint described, and write down the result, not "OK":

- **Re-test the reading that pointed to the cause.** If a pressure test found the leak, the correction records the new pressure test result: no leak.
- **Recreate the complaint's conditions.** A shutdown under load is verified by a run under load; a loss of power on grades by a loaded road test.
- **Check that the codes stay gone.** Clear them, run the unit through the conditions that set them, and confirm none come back active or pending.
- **Record what you saw.** A reading, a run time, a photo of the gauge. The next technician and the customer can read a result. They can't read "checked."

## What a work order needs around the 3 C's

The 3 C's are the story of the job. The rest of the work order makes that story traceable:

- **Equipment identification:** unit or asset number, VIN or serial number, and the hour meter or odometer reading at the visit.
- **The customer and the authorization:** who the work is for, and who approved it.
- **Date and technician:** when the work happened and who did it.
- **Labor and parts:** time on the job and the parts used, so the write-up and the invoice agree.
- **Photos and readings** that support the cause or the verification.
- **What comes next:** deferred items and any follow-up the customer should schedule.

## Common write-up mistakes

- **"Checked and OK."** It says nothing about what was checked or how.
- **A cause that repeats the complaint.** "Engine overheating due to overheating" is not a diagnosis.
- **A correction with no verification.** The part was replaced, but nobody confirmed the complaint is gone.
- **Missing deferred work.** The item you found and did not fix is the one the customer most needs to know about.

## Why the write-up matters

Your customer sees the equipment come back, not the work. The write-up is how they see what was found and what was fixed, and it is what the next technician reads before they start. A clear complaint, cause, and correction lets the work speak for itself.

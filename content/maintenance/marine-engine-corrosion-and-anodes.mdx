**On a marine engine, some metal is meant to wear away.** Sacrificial anodes, often called zincs whatever they are made of, corrode in place of the metal around them. When they are gone, the engine, the heat exchanger, and the fittings start doing that job instead. That is why marine diesel PM gives corrosion and anodes a section of their own.

This page describes corrosion and anodes at the category level. Where anodes are fitted, what they are made of, and when they need replacing come from the engine manufacturer's documentation and the water the boat operates in.

## Why marine engines corrode

Salt water conducts electricity, and dissimilar metals in contact with it set up a small battery that eats the less noble metal. Raw-water cooling brings that water through the heat exchanger and the exhaust. Fresh water and brackish water behave differently, and a boat that moves between them changes the picture again.

## Where it shows up

Corrosion appears on the engine's own anodes, on the fittings that carry seawater, and on the outside of the block and manifolds. The exhaust elbow, where hot exhaust meets cooling water, is a common wear point; see [air intake and exhaust](/kb/diesel-air-intake-and-exhaust-maintenance). The raw-water circuit is covered under [cooling system maintenance](/kb/diesel-engine-cooling-system-maintenance).

## What the customer's record should show

A boat may sit for months between visits, and the person who receives the record may not be the person who uses the boat. The corrosion section should say what was inspected, what condition it was found in, what was replaced, and what to watch before the next visit. Photos are especially useful here, because they show the next technician the condition at the last visit. See [what belongs in a PM report for the customer](/kb/what-belongs-in-a-pm-report-for-the-customer).

For the full visit, see the [marine diesel engine PM checklist](/kb/marine-diesel-engine-maintenance-checklist).

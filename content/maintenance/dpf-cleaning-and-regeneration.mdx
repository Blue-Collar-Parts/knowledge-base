**A diesel particulate filter (DPF) is cleaned by taking it off the vehicle or equipment: the ash left behind after regeneration is removed on a dedicated cleaning machine, or the filter is exchanged for a cleaned or new unit, following the manufacturer's procedure for that filter.** Regeneration, which burns soot off in place, is a separate job; see [DPF cleaning versus regeneration](/kb/dpf-cleaning-vs-regeneration). This page is the map of DPF service. Each linked page goes deeper on one part of it.

## How is a DPF cleaned?

The manufacturer's service documentation for the specific engine and filter sets the interval, the acceptable ash load, and the cleaning method; it is not something to estimate by mileage or feel alone.

A filter that regenerates normally can still be filling with ash. To tell a filter that needs another regeneration from one that needs cleaning, see [DPF cleaning versus regeneration](/kb/dpf-cleaning-vs-regeneration), which also explains passive, active and parked regeneration. For what to capture when a filter is serviced, see [recording a DPF service](/kb/recording-a-dpf-service).

## What the DPF does

Diesel exhaust carries soot: fine particulate from combustion. The DPF is a honeycomb-structured filter, usually ceramic, that sits downstream of the engine and catches that soot as exhaust passes through. Left alone, a filter that keeps collecting soot and never sheds it would plug solid and choke the engine. Regeneration is how the system keeps that from happening; the mineral ash that remains is what cleaning removes.

## How the aftertreatment system fits together

The DPF doesn't work alone. On a modern diesel, EGR works at the engine and the aftertreatment system treats the exhaust after it leaves:

1. **EGR (exhaust gas recirculation)**, at the engine, before the exhaust reaches the aftertreatment, routes some exhaust back into the intake to lower combustion temperatures and reduce the formation of NOx in the first place. See [EGR cooler failure](/kb/egr-cooler-replacement).
2. **DOC (diesel oxidation catalyst)** sits ahead of the DPF and oxidizes CO, unburned fuel, and hydrocarbons, and it raises exhaust temperature to help the DPF regenerate.
3. **DPF** traps soot, as described above.
4. **SCR (selective catalytic reduction) with DEF (diesel exhaust fluid)** sits downstream of the DPF and converts NOx into nitrogen and water using DEF injected into the exhaust stream. See [DEF and SCR basics](/kb/def-and-scr-basics).

A problem anywhere in that chain — a failing EGR cooler, DEF quality, a DPF overdue for cleaning — can show up as a fault or a derate that looks like it belongs somewhere else. Diagnosing it means checking the whole chain, not just the component throwing the code.

## What this page does not cover

This page and its related pages describe how the system works and what a service provider documents. They don't give make- or model-specific regeneration temperatures, ash load limits, or cleaning intervals — those come from the engine or aftertreatment manufacturer's own service documentation for the specific engine and filter. They also don't cover modifying, removing, or disabling any part of this system; that isn't maintenance, and it isn't covered here.
